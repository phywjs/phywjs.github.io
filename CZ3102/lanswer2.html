Sample Answers to Lab 2

Q1

(a)

Given the data for stars, we are asked to make a plot of the stars. First, we need to understand the meaning of the data in the file stars.dat. It is noted that the locations of the stars are specified in angles. The coordinate system is fixed relative to the stars, not fixed to Earth, thus the position of the stars do not change. Imaging the stars are on a sphere of radius 1, with Earth (the point of observation) at the center (0,0,0). The stars are specified by two angles, the Right Ascension (RA) and declination (Dec). RA and Dec are slightly different from usual mathematical convention of spherical polar system. The RA specifies the azimuth angle phi, but using 24 hours for 360 degree (or 2pi radian). For declination, the north pole is +90 degree, and south pole is -90 degree, and at the equator it is 0 degree. For convenience, we convert RA (hour/minute/sec) and Dec (degree/minute/sec) to radian for the spherical polar coordinates (theta, and phi) as

theta = (PI/180.0)*(90.0 - sign*(DECdeg + DECmin/60.0 + DECsec/3600.0))

phi = 2.0*PI*(RAhr + RAmin/60.0 + RAsec/3600.0)/24.0

We can convert from spherical polar coordinates (r, theta, phi) to Cartesian coordinates (x,y,z), the relation is

x = r*sin(theta)*cos(phi)

y = r*sin(theta)*sin(phi)

z = r*cos(theta)

The inverse from Cartesian to spherical polar system is then

r = sqrt(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)

phi = arctan(y,x)

theta = arccos(z/r)

We note that the stars are located on a sphere. We view the stars from inside. Here I offer several possibilities to "draw" the stars.

(a) a 3D plot (x,y,z), in such a plot, we are viewing from outside, rather than inside.

(b) a 2D plot of RA vs Dec (or theta vs phi), this is quick and easy, but the locations are distorted, particularly near the poles. [Imaging we flattern out a curved sphere onto a flat surface].

(c) a 2D plot, projected somehow from 3D to 2D according to the principle of image in a camera. In fact, this is not that complicated. The light rays converge from stars to a single focus point. A film is before or after the focus point. The location of the image of the stars are the intersections between the light ray and the plane of film. In the simplest case, we can set the focus point at (-infinity, 0, 0) and the film at the plane z=0. Thus, the star at (x,y,z) is projected on to (x,y,0). But this means we are only looking at in the direction of north pole or south pole. To be more general, we specify a view angle (in terms of RA and Dec) so that the center of the camera is pointing to that particular location in the sky. We still assume the film plane passes through the origin (0,0,0). The focus point is located a distance d from the origin. In order to do this, we need to rotate the coordinate system so that new axis z' is in the specified direction. See the code for details.

There is the question of how to draw the plot. I decided to generate directly a PostScript file. Using PostScript allows me to have a fine control on the size of the dot based on the visual magnitude Vmag. Thus dimmer stars are plotted with smaller dots. Note that smaller number (negative or 1) means brighter stars, bigger number (say 5) means fainter stars. I also drew the RA/Dec grid, this help orient the viewer. A C program is linked here (map.c).

Clearly, the above implementation is still rather primitive. For example, one should label the brightest stars by their names, or one can draw the constellation lines, or one should be able to zoom-in or zoom-out. To get a feel of a very good star map, you are welcome to have a look of the site http://www.skymap.com/. The free evaluation version run on Windows is an excellent software for star gazers.
