Q1

(a) By rescaling t with t'=ct, we can get rid of the constant g/L. In the new variable t', the derivatives are now related by

dq/dt = dq/dt'*dt'/dt = c*dq/dt'

d^2q/dt^2 = c^2 *dq/dt'^2

Thus, if we take c^2 = g/L, i.e., c = sqrt(g/L), then the equation becomes dimensionless

d^2 q/dt'^2 + sin(q) = 0

(b) most students can draw something similar to Fig.5.11. The point is that we need certain number of different initial conditions to cover the (q, dq/dt) space. Please do label the curve to indicate the direction of the flow.

Actually, we can draw these curves without solving the equations, we can use the energy conservation equation. But this can be down only if the energy is conserved. The current method of solving the equation is more general.

(c) There are three type of distinct regions in the phase space, (i) these close to (0,0) [or (2k*pi,0), k = ..., -2,-1,0,1,...], and (ii) these at (pi,0) [or translated in q by multiple of 2pi], and (iii) these with large enough |dq/dt|. Case (i) is near a stable fixed point (0,0), although it is not an attractor. Here the pendulum makes small amplitude oscillations. Case (ii) is near a unstable fixed point. (pi,0) is an equilibrium point, but any small perturbation renders the pendulum moving away from it. Note that the pendulum is in the upper vertical direction. Case (iii) corresponds to high velocity such that the pendulum is rotating counter-clockwise (if dq/dt>0) or clockwise (if dq/dt<0) about origin. Here it is, of course, not in equilibrium.

(d) If we have two solutions with initial conditions that are very close to each other, it will remain close until the time is so large that the two solutions are out of phase (due to different periods of the solutions). But for pendulum, it is nearly always true that

| q_1(t) - q_2(t) | < c *delta

for sufficiently small delta and any finite time interval. where c should be a constant independent of delta. This means that if we reduce delta by a factor of 10, the error should decrease by a factor of 10. The move of the pendulum is not chaotic.

Q2

(a) In the Lorenz equation, it is useful to look at where are the fixed points (if any), and whether they are stable. From this, we can classify the general behavior of the equation. For small r, the typical case is approach to stable equilibrium. For very large r it may approach a limit cycle [periodic solution]. For intermediate range, it is very irregular. There are two unstable fixed points, but the motion is attracted to both of them. It dances around them here and there. This is known as 'strange' attractor.

(b) In the case of approaching to stable fixed point, the error decrease to 0 between two solutions. However, in the so-called chaotic region, no matter how small you start with a delta, the error becomes many times larger after some time t, thus the error cannot be bounded by delta. After that time t, we practically loss predictability. The solution is so sensitive to the initial condition that any small error leads to completely different result.
