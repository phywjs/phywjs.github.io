A few remarks on Q#2: Accuracy of calculating e^x with the Taylor Series
------------------------------------------------------------------------
There are a few reasons why you have inaccurate answers compared with
results obtained from the calculator.

1) Results are at most accurate to only 5 dec. places since the stopping
   condition is |y|<0.00001.

2) A further limitation is that the single-precision floating point 
   representation is only accurate to at most 7 significant figures.
   (This is because there are only 23 bits for the mantissa)

3) Another factor comes from using the Taylor series to calculate 
   results. For positive x, the results are as expected given the
   limitations (1) & (2) above.

   For large and negative x, however, the accuracy drops drastically.
   To see why, we have to examine the individual terms of the series.
   The short answer is that negative x produces terms of alternating
   signs, and large x produces individual terms that are very large.
   But the final result is supposed to be very small or very close
   to zero. By adding very large terms (large in comparison with
   the final result) of opposite sign, there is loss of significance
   because of the limitations of the single-prec float. pt.
   representation. I won't go into detail here though...

   If you are interested in more detail, please refer to any 
   Numerical Analysis/Methods book on the topic of errors with the
   floating point representation. There are some available in the 
   lab.

4) Some of you have also observed that by changing the order of
   evaluating y, the accuracy changes. This is related to (3) above.

   Doing y = y * (x/n) produces a more accurate result than
   y=(y*x)/n. Calculating (x/n) first makes this part of the product
   small in magnitude, so the individual term, y, does not grow as
   large as calculating (y*x) first, since x is large.
